### CHAPTER 1 — THE MATH OF THE TRAP (≈2:30)

`[VISUAL: J.P. Morgan "Agony & Ecstasy" distribution graphic]` `[SRC: J.P. Morgan, "The Agony and the Ecstasy," 2014; 2024 update]`

Start with the base rate, because your employer's stock feels special and the data says it probably isn't. J.P. Morgan's long-running study of every stock that entered the Russell 3000 since 1980 found two things. Roughly two-thirds of them underperformed the index itself. And about forty percent suffered what they call a catastrophic loss — a decline of seventy percent or more from the peak, that they never recovered from. The 2024 update puts that catastrophic share at forty-four percent through 2020.

`[BEAT]` Not forty percent of penny stocks. Forty percent of the investable universe.

Hendrik Bessembinder's research at Arizona State goes further: from 1926 to 2016, just four percent of all listed U.S. companies — about eleven hundred names — accounted for the entire net wealth creation of the American stock market. The other ninety-six percent, collectively, matched Treasury bills. `[SRC: Bessembinder, "Do Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills?", JFE 2018]`

`[TONE: even-handed]` Now, the other side — because this channel does not do one-sided. Concentration is also how the winners won. Ballmer is richer than Gates precisely because he concentrated. Pick the right stock, hold it for forty years, and diversification is the mistake. The problem is not that concentration never works. The problem is that you cannot know, in advance, which side of the distribution you are on — and the downside is not "underperform by two percent." The downside is Enron. It is UnitedHealth.

`[VISUAL: UNH price chart, Sept 2024 → early 2026, −50%+ shaded]` `[SRC: Cache illustrative case study (UNH); Yahoo Finance / STAT, May 13, 2025]`

UnitedHealth was a darling compounder for a decade. Between late 2024 and early 2026 it lost more than half its value — a CEO stepping down, a pulled forecast, a reported federal investigation. If you were a UnitedHealth executive with seventy percent of your net worth in UNH, the twenty-four percent you "saved" by not selling looks very small next to a fifty-percent drawdown. The tax you were avoiding was a rounding error against the risk you were carrying.

`[BEAT]` And yet the tax is real. On a ten-million-dollar position with near-zero basis — founder stock, early RSUs that vested at a fraction of today's price — you owe twenty percent federal long-term capital gains, plus the three-point-eight percent net investment income tax. That is twenty-three-point-eight percent before your state takes a bite. In California, call it thirty-seven. In New York City, the high thirties. On ten million, roughly three-point-seven million dollars out the door. `[SRC: IRC §1(h), §1411; CA FTB / NY DTF top rates]`

So both things are true at once. The risk of holding is enormous and probabilistic. The cost of selling is large and certain. That tension is the entire subject.

Here is the statistic that should reframe it. BlackRock's inaugural Advisor Trends Survey — over a thousand U.S. advisors, fielded by Escalent in late 2025 — found that sixty-three percent of millennial advisors courting wealthy clients now offer concentrated-stock solutions, versus fifty-five percent of Gen X and fifty-three percent of Boomers. `[SRC: BlackRock Advisor Trends Survey 2025; InvestmentNews, Jan 14, 2026]` The younger advisors get it. But the same survey found something damning: ninety-two percent of advisors serving the wealthy say they are asked for tax guidance — yet only seventeen percent treat after-tax return as a primary driver of portfolio decisions. The industry is selling tax-awareness and delivering tax-avoidance-as-paralysis.

`[TONE: the reframe, slow]` So let me give you the peer-reviewed version of "just pay the tax." In a 2025 study in the Journal of Asset Management, BlackRock's researchers backtested three hundred and eighty historical ten-year paths. Their finding: selling the whole position immediately and reinvesting in the index performed, on average, about in line with holding the stock — after tax. `[SRC: "Loss harvesting strategies tax efficiently diversify concentrated stock," J. Asset Management, July 2025]` The tax bill you are terrified of was, on average, paid for by the risk you removed.

That does not mean always sell everything today. It means the tax is the price of the exit, not a reason to stay in the building. And it means the sophisticated strategies we are about to cover have to beat a very simple benchmark: pay the tax, buy the index, sleep at night.

Six exits. From the one your compliance department already knows, to the one your family office whispers about.

### EXIT 1 — THE 10b5-1 PLAN: DISCIPLINE, NOT TAX AVOIDANCE (≈6:40)

`[VISUAL: SEC Form 4 screenshot, 10b5-1 checkbox highlighted]` `[SRC: SEC Rule 10b5-1; Dec 2022 amendments]`

The first exit is the one most executives already have, and most misuse. A 10b5-1 plan is a pre-scheduled, written instruction to sell shares on a set schedule or at set prices, adopted at a time when you do not possess material non-public information. It removes your discretion. That is the whole point. It is an affirmative defense against insider-trading liability — not a tax strategy. You pay full capital gains on every share it sells.

Why start here? Because it is the baseline every other strategy is measured against, and because the discipline is the feature.

`[VISUAL: Amazon Form 4 / press headline, Aug 2026]` `[SRC: Yahoo Finance, Aug 6, 2026; Barron's, Feb 2, 2024]`

Jeff Bezos sold roughly four billion dollars of Amazon in August 2026 — through a plan he adopted on November fourteenth, 2025. The sale was scheduled before the earnings beat that preceded it. He did not time it. The plan timed it. That is what a correctly used plan looks like: you commit in advance, and you let the schedule execute regardless of what you know or feel later. His earlier 2024 plan authorized up to fifty million shares — about eight-point-six billion dollars.

`[TONE: shift, cautionary]` Now the trap. In December 2022 the SEC amended the rule, effective 2023, because executives were abusing it. For directors and officers, trading cannot begin until the later of ninety days after adoption, or two business days after the company files the quarterly or annual report covering the quarter you adopted in — capped at a hundred and twenty days. Overlapping plans for the same class of stock are generally banned. You must certify, in writing, that you are acting in good faith and hold no material non-public information. And the company must disclose plan adoptions and terminations in its 10-Qs and 10-Ks; you check a box on your Form 4. `[SRC: SEC Release 33-11138, Dec 14, 2022]`

`[BEAT]` The plan is no longer a quiet autopilot. It is a publicly filed legal instrument, and regulators run data-driven screens on it.

Which brings me to the CEO I promised you. Terren Peizer ran a healthcare company called Ontrak. He adopted 10b5-1 plans while knowing a major customer was about to terminate its contract — information the market did not have. The plans sold before the news broke, avoiding more than twelve-and-a-half million dollars in losses. In June 2024 a jury convicted him of securities fraud and two counts of insider trading — the first criminal conviction based solely on the misuse of a 10b5-1 plan. He was sentenced to forty-two months and a five-and-a-quarter-million-dollar fine. `[SRC: DOJ, June 23, 2025; U.S. v. Peizer, C.D. Cal.]`

`[TONE: the both-sides beat]` And here is the part that makes this a real story instead of a morality play: in January 2026, President Trump pardoned him. The SEC dropped its parallel civil case that August. `[SRC: Reuters, Aug 7, 2026]` The conviction did not stand. But the legal architecture did. The 2022 amendments, the cooling-off periods, the certifications, the disclosure regime — none of that was undone by a pardon. And two years before Peizer, the SEC had already settled insider-trading charges against Cheetah Mobile's CEO and former president for setting up plans while holding negative revenue information. `[SRC: SEC Press Release 2022-169, Sept 21, 2022]` The enforcement pattern is the point, not the pardon.

`[VISUAL: "WRONG ANSWER" card]`

So: the kill condition. A 10b5-1 plan is the wrong answer if you think it lets you trade on what you know. It is the wrong answer if you need the tax gone — it does nothing for the tax. And it is the wrong answer if you cannot commit to a schedule, because modifying or canceling a plan while holding bad news is itself evidence of bad faith.

What it is right for: new vests and option exercises you want to convert to cash on autopilot, with legal cover, while you decide what to do with the existing block. It is the conveyor belt. The next five strategies are what you do with the warehouse.

`[TONE: transition, lighter]` And the first of those requires a seven-year marriage.

### EXIT 2 — EXCHANGE FUNDS: THE SEVEN-YEAR MARRIAGE (≈10:40)

`[VISUAL: Diagram — many investors contribute single stocks → one partnership → each owns a slice of the whole basket]` `[SRC: IRC §721; §704(c)(1)(B); §737]`

An exchange fund is a private partnership. You contribute your appreciated stock; a hundred other investors contribute theirs; in return you own a pro-rata slice of the entire pooled basket. Under Section 721 of the tax code, contributing property to a partnership is not a sale. No immediate tax. You have swapped single-stock risk for broad-market risk in one transaction — instantly, not over a decade of selling.

When you eventually redeem, you receive a basket of diversified securities carrying your original, low cost basis. The gain is deferred until you sell those securities. And if you hold until death, your heirs get a stepped-up basis — the deferred gain can be eliminated entirely. That estate-planning angle is the quiet superpower.

`[TONE: the catch, deliberate]` The catch is the seven years. To keep the tax treatment, you must stay in the fund for at least seven years — a rule built from Sections 704(c) and 737 to stop people using these as tax-free stock-swap machines. Leave early and you typically get your original contributed stock back — the exact concentration you were trying to escape — plus exit fees of one to two percent. Some modern funds have no formal lock-up, but the economics of early exit are designed to hurt. This is not a parking spot. It is a marriage.

There is a second structural quirk. To qualify for the tax treatment, the fund cannot be a pure "investment company" — so it must hold at least twenty percent of assets in illiquid things like real estate. That is twenty percent of your money in assets you did not choose, added to satisfy a tax definition.

`[VISUAL: Provider comparison table]` `[SRC: Cache product docs; Embark Funds 2026 guide; Morgan Stanley exchange fund overview]`

Now the real-world terms, because this is where the marketing and the math diverge. The legacy providers — Eaton Vance, Goldman Sachs — have historically required qualified-purchaser status, meaning five million in investments, with minimums around five hundred thousand to a million, and fees that can run from seventy basis points to two percent a year. The newer entrant, Cache, opened it to accredited investors at a hundred-thousand-dollar minimum, with fees starting at zero-point-nine-five percent and stepping down to zero-point-four-zero at twenty-five million.

`[TONE: skeptical, fair]` And Cache publishes performance. Its UNIX fund, benchmarked to the Nasdaq-100, returned a net thirty-nine-point-three-two percent from its August 2024 inception through September 2025, versus twenty-seven-point-one-one for the index — twelve points of alpha. `[SRC: Cache Q3 2025 Official Results, provider-reported]` Impressive. Now read the fine print with me. That is a provider-reported number on a track record barely a year long. And the outperformance came from early-quarter overweights in Nvidia, Apple, Broadcom and Google — the same mega-cap tech you already own. That is active risk-taking, not structural magic. It can reverse.

`[VISUAL: Tracking-error drift graphic]`

The deeper issue is tracking error, and it is structural. The fund cannot freely sell the stocks investors contributed — selling would trigger tax for everyone. So the basket drifts from the index. Contributed sectors stay overweight. Cache's own Q3 report shows its S&P fund running about one-point-six percent tracking error in its first quarter; the drift compounds over the seven years you are locked in. You are paying up to two percent a year for something that is not quite the index, cannot be customized, and cannot be left.

`[VISUAL: "WRONG ANSWER" card]`

The kill conditions, straight from the advisor literature: you need liquidity inside seven years — wrong answer. Your stock has little embedded gain, so there is little tax to defer — wrong answer, the fees eat you. Or you have genuine, high conviction your stock beats the index for a decade — then concentration is a deliberate choice, and a broad-market fund is structurally the wrong tool.

Right for: a large, highly appreciated position, no liquidity need for seven-plus years, and the humility to admit you do not know if your stock is the next Apple or the next Enron. That humility is the actual product.

### EXIT 3 — DIRECT INDEXING: TAX ALPHA, OR AN EXPENSIVE ETF? (≈14:45)

`[VISUAL: S&P 500 ETF → explodes into 500 individual holdings; losers highlighted red, harvested]` `[SRC: IRC §1091 wash-sale rule]`

Direct indexing means owning the individual stocks of an index in your own account, instead of one fund share. Same exposure. But now you can harvest losses one stock at a time. In any given year, even when the index is up, some constituents are down. You sell the losers, book the loss, and buy a similar — not "substantially identical" — replacement to stay invested. Those losses offset the capital gains you generate selling your concentrated position. That is the entire pitch: manufacture a stream of tax losses to pay for your diversification.

The mechanics live and die by the wash-sale rule. Sell a stock at a loss, and you cannot buy it back for thirty-one days without disallowing the loss. So the manager swaps in a correlated substitute and waits. Do this across hundreds of names, year-round, and you get what the industry calls "tax alpha."

`[VISUAL: Adoption stats]` `[SRC: Cerulli Associates, April 2025; FTSE Russell Direct Indexing Survey 2026]`

It is not a niche. Cerulli counted eight hundred sixty-four billion dollars in direct-indexing assets at the end of 2024 — nearly double 2021. FTSE Russell's 2026 survey found eighty-three percent of advisors using it or planning to within twelve months.

`[TONE: the turn]` Now the other side, and this is where most YouTube videos stop selling and start thinking. The tax alpha is real but modest, front-loaded, and assumption-dependent. Published estimates land in the low single digits of percent per year — biggest in the early years and in choppy markets, shrinking as the account appreciates and fewer positions sit below basis. Frec's own simulation models about fourteen-point-seven percent of capital harvested as losses in year one, tapering toward forty percent cumulative over a decade. Those are hypothetical, provider-modeled numbers. `[SRC: Frec, "Is the tax alpha worth the fee?", Oct 2026]`

And the fee is the variable that decides everything. Legacy providers charged sixty to a hundred-ten basis points, sometimes more — and you pay those fees with after-tax dollars, because individual investment-advisory fees are not deductible. Elm Wealth's analysis is blunt: at legacy pricing, the fees can consume the tax benefit entirely. Their April 2026 follow-up on levered long/short direct indexing found fees ate more than half the tax benefit over a twenty-year model. `[SRC: Elm Wealth, "Is the Juice Worth the Squeeze?", 2024; "Robbing Peter to Pay Paul," April 2026]`

`[VISUAL: Fee compression table — Frec 0.09% / Schwab 0.40% / legacy 0.60–1.10%+]` `[SRC: Frec; Schwab Personalized Indexing]`

But here is the 2026 update the old articles miss: fee compression. Frec now charges as little as zero-point-zero-nine percent with a twenty-thousand-dollar minimum. Schwab's Personalized Indexing starts at zero-point-four-zero. The strategy that was a fee trap at one percent is a different proposition at nine basis points.

`[TONE: the save-worthy framework]` So here is the napkin test — write this down. Your extra fee, divided by your marginal tax rate, equals the losses you must harvest just to break even. On a million dollars, a nine-basis-point fee versus a three-basis-point ETF is a six-hundred-dollar gap. At a thirty-five percent rate, you need about seventeen hundred dollars of usable losses a year to cover it. Harvest more than that, and the fee pays for itself. The word "usable" carries the weight — a loss only helps if you have gains to offset, or you use the three-thousand-dollar ordinary-income allowance; the rest carries forward. `[SRC: Frec break-even framework; IRS Topic 409]`

`[VISUAL: "WRONG ANSWER" card]`

Kill conditions: you have no regular capital gains to offset — the losses become a carryforward museum. The account is an IRA — tax-loss harvesting is worthless there. You are cost-sensitive and the fee gap is wide. Or you want low maintenance — managing hundreds of positions and wash-sale windows is not low maintenance.

And one structural critique worth knowing: an S&P 500 direct index owns today's five hundred largest companies. Bessembinder's four-percent finding means the next generation of wealth creators will not all come from today's index members. A segmented basket of low-cost sector and international ETFs can generate similar losses with broader coverage and less tracking error. The "sophisticated" choice is not automatically the better one.

`[TONE: pattern interrupt, lean in]` Notice what just happened. The advanced strategy might be worse than a three-basis-point ETF. The next one is worse than that in a bull market — and it once saved Mark Cuban a billion dollars. Both things are true. Let me show you.

### EXIT 4 — PROTECTIVE COLLARS: THE CUBAN TRADE AND ITS FINE PRINT (≈18:55)

`[VISUAL: 1999 Yahoo / Broadcast.com headline; Cuban photo]` `[SRC: Business Insider / Real Vision interview, June 2020]`

April 1999. Yahoo buys Mark Cuban's Broadcast.com for five-point-seven billion dollars in stock. Cuban receives fourteen-point-six million Yahoo shares, worth about one-point-four billion at ninety-five dollars a share. He cannot sell immediately — lock-up. And he thinks the market is insane. Quote: "In the eighties I watched PC companies go straight up and then come straight down. I was like, it is going to happen again."

So he goes to Goldman Sachs and builds a collar. He buys put options with an eighty-five-dollar strike — a floor, the right to sell at eighty-five no matter how far the stock falls. He sells call options with a two-hundred-and-five-dollar strike — a ceiling, an obligation to sell at two-oh-five if it rockets. The premium he collects from the calls pays for the puts. Net cost: approximately zero. He could not even put the collar on for six months, so he bought puts outright first — quote, "literally every single penny I had."

`[VISUAL: Yahoo price chart 1999–2002, $95 → $230+ → collapse; collar band $85–$205 shaded]`

Yahoo went above two-thirty by January 2000. Cuban's ceiling capped his upside — he "lost" the rally above two-oh-five. Then the bubble burst and Yahoo collapsed. His floor held. Quote: "My hedge worked out perfectly." The collar did not make him rich. It kept him rich. That is the entire purpose of this tool: convert an unbounded outcome into a known range. You are not buying return. You are buying a floor.

`[TONE: the other side, immediate]` Now the cost, because a collar is not free just because the premium nets to zero. The cost is the upside you surrender. And in a sustained bull market, that cost is enormous.

`[VISUAL: XCLR vs SPY cumulative 2022–2025]` `[SRC: PortfoliosLab total-return data; Global X XCLR]`

Look at the Global X S&P 500 Collar 95-110 ETF — a real, listed fund that mechanically collars the index. In 2022 it lost twelve-point-nine percent while the S&P lost eighteen-point-two. The floor worked. But over the full 2022-through-2025 cycle it returned roughly thirty-four percent against the S&P's fifty-two. The insurance paid out in the bear market and then charged you the rally as its premium. A collar is a tactical bridge, not a destination.

`[VISUAL: Tax-code warning card]` `[SRC: IRC §1259 constructive sales; §1092 straddle rules]`

Then the tax minefield, and this is where people get hurt. First, constructive sales. Under Section 1259, if your hedge eliminates substantially all of your risk of loss and opportunity for gain, the IRS treats it as if you sold the stock — immediate tax on the entire embedded gain, the exact thing you were avoiding. There is no official safe harbor. Practitioners often want a band of roughly fifteen percent or more between floor and ceiling, but that is a rule of thumb, not law. It is facts-and-circumstances, and it carries audit risk.

Second, the straddle rules under Section 1092. If your call expires unexercised, the premium you collected is short-term capital gain — taxable now. But the cost of your expired put, the loss, can be deferred until you close the whole position. You can owe tax on the income while being barred from deducting the matching expense. Practitioners call it the whipsaw. Some use a single over-the-counter contract combining both legs to mitigate this — which introduces dealer counterparty risk that listed, OCC-cleared options do not carry.

`[BEAT]` And the practical killer: most public-company insider-trading policies explicitly prohibit executives from hedging company stock at all. Before you model a single strike price, read your company's policy. For many of you, this exit is locked from the inside.

`[VISUAL: "WRONG ANSWER" card]`

Kill conditions: you are bullish — you will resent the ceiling every day. You need long-term diversification — a collar hedges, it does not diversify. Your employer prohibits hedging. Or you cannot tolerate the tax complexity.

Right for: a highly convicted holder of a volatile stock, who needs a floor for a defined window — say, while waiting out a lock-up or an election — whose employer permits it, and who understands they are renting protection, not owning a solution.

`[TONE: transition]` Which brings us to the only strategy that can make the capital-gains tax disappear entirely. And the reason you can never get the money back.

### EXIT 5 — CHARITABLE VEHICLES: THE BEST MATH YOU CANNOT UNDO (≈23:10)

`[VISUAL: Diagram — appreciated stock → CRT/DAF → sold tax-free → diversified portfolio; income stream / grants out]` `[SRC: IRC §664; §170; §7520]`

Here is the mechanism that beats everything else on after-tax dollars, if — and only if — you were going to give anyway. You donate appreciated stock directly to a charitable vehicle. You do not sell it first. The vehicle sells it, and because it is tax-exempt, no capital-gains tax is triggered. The full pre-tax value goes to work. You also get an income-tax deduction. The gain you spent years deferring is not deferred. It is gone.

Two structures. A donor-advised fund is the simple one: an account at a public charity — Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard. You contribute the stock, take an immediate deduction at fair market value, the fund sells tax-free, and you recommend grants over time. No income stream back to you. It is a charitable checking account with a tax engine.

A charitable remainder trust is the complex one. Irrevocable. You fund it with the stock; it sells tax-free and reinvests the full proceeds; it pays you an income stream for a term of years or for life; and whatever remains at the end goes to charity. You get a deduction now for the present value of that future remainder — calculated using the IRS Section 7520 rate, which as of November 2026 sits around six percent, high by post-2008 standards, and a higher rate means a bigger deduction. `[SRC: IRS §7520 rates, Rev. Rul. 2026-21; check current month]` The income you receive is taxed in a specific order: ordinary income first, then capital gains, then tax-free income, then return of principal. And the trust must pass a ten-percent minimum-remainder test and avoid unrelated-business taxable income — which means no borrowing on margin inside it.

`[VISUAL: Valur comparison bar chart]` `[SRC: Valur, "Tax Deferral Strategy: Comparing the Big Three," updated Nov 2026 — illustrative]`

Now the numbers, with the assumptions stated, because this is a vendor illustration and I will label it as one. Valur models a thirty-five-year-old in California with a one-million-dollar, zero-basis asset, a thirty-five percent tax rate, and ten-percent annual market growth. After ten years, on a net-present-value basis: no tax planning, about one-point-three-seven million. An exchange fund, about one-point-five-nine. A charitable remainder trust, about two-point-one-five. `[BEAT]` The CRT wins by a wide margin — lower cost structure, longer deferral, plus the deduction.

`[TONE: the essential caveat, slow]` But read what "wins" means. The remainder — the principal that did not get paid out to you — goes to charity. Permanently. This is not a tax trick that lets you keep the money. It is the optimal way to give money you had already decided to give. If you do not have genuine philanthropic intent, the CRT is not the best strategy. It is a one-way door you should not walk through.

`[VISUAL: 2026 rule-change card]` `[SRC: OBBBA 2025; Fidelity Charitable; Journal of Accountancy, Oct 2025]`

And here is the 2026 change almost nobody is pricing in. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act added, starting this year, a half-percent-of-AGI floor on itemized charitable deductions — you lose the deduction on the first half-percent of your adjusted gross income. And for taxpayers in the thirty-seven-percent bracket, the value of itemized charitable deductions is capped at thirty-five cents on the dollar, not thirty-seven. The deduction got slightly less valuable exactly as these strategies got more popular. The appreciated-asset deduction is still generally limited to thirty percent of AGI, with a five-year carryforward. Model the current rules, not the 2023 blog post.

Two more traps. The contribution is irrevocable — once it is in, it is out of your estate, forever. And timing: if you donate stock that is already subject to a binding sale agreement, the IRS can invoke the anticipatory-assignment-of-income doctrine and tax you on the sale anyway. Donate before a liquidity event is legally locked, and get a qualified appraisal for non-public stock.

`[VISUAL: "WRONG ANSWER" card]`

Kill conditions: no real philanthropic intent. You might need the principal. You want flexibility. Any of those, and this is the wrong door.

`[TONE: founder aside, quick]` One aside for the founders watching, because it can dwarf everything else in this video. If your stock is private and qualifies as Qualified Small Business Stock under Section 1202, the 2025 law created tiered exclusions for stock acquired after July fourth, 2025: fifty percent of the gain excluded at three years, seventy-five at four, a hundred at five — with a fifteen-million-dollar per-issuer cap and a seventy-five-million-dollar gross-asset test. Older stock keeps the old ten-million, five-year, hundred-percent regime. If you qualify, read Section 1202 before you read anything else. It can make the entire tax question vanish. `[SRC: OBBBA 2025; Holland & Knight, July 2025]`

### EXIT 6 — LEVERED LONG/SHORT: THE FAMILY-OFFICE TOOL (≈27:40)

`[VISUAL: 130/30 and 200/100 structure diagram — long extension + short extension around the concentrated core]` `[SRC: J. Asset Management, July 2025; BlackRock]`

The last exit is the one your family office whispers about. A levered long/short program synthetically replaces your concentrated position with broad-market exposure, using leverage. You run a long extension — additional securities bought with borrowed funds — and a short extension — borrowed securities sold — constructed to track an index while generating losses that offset the gains from unwinding your stock. The 130/30 version runs a hundred-thirty percent long, thirty short. The aggressive 200/100 runs two hundred long, a hundred short. The goal: tax-neutral diversification, fast.

`[VISUAL: Active-weight decay curves, LS200 vs GB5]` `[SRC: "Loss harvesting strategies…," J. Asset Management 26:447–463, 2025]`

The evidence is the best of any strategy here, because it is peer-reviewed and open-access. BlackRock's researchers ran three hundred and eighty ten-year historical paths. The 200/100 program reduced the concentrated position's active weight to five percent or less on one hundred percent of paths by year ten — and ninety-one percent by year five. A slow, sell-five-percent-a-year gains-budget approach hit that target on only twenty-two percent of paths by year ten. Tracking error fell below five percent on every path by year three. And the average after-tax active return versus simply holding the stock was positive one-point-three-four percent a year — net of transaction costs, financing, and management fees. Cumulative tax drag over the decade: effectively zero.

`[TONE: the other side, the correction]` Now the cost, and I am going to correct a number that appears in some summaries of this research. The deferral is not forgiveness. In the published results, liquidating the 200/100 portfolio at the ten-year mark cost a median of about twenty-one-and-a-half percent of the portfolio's pre-liquidation value in tax — versus about fifteen-point-eight percent for the pay-as-you-go approach. You traded a stream of small tax bills for one large one at the exit. The strategy that defers the most tax tends to owe the most at the end. And if you bequeath or donate the portfolio, that exit tax can be zero — which loops us right back to the estate and charitable exits.

`[VISUAL: GE / JNJ / AAPL 2007–2017 case study]`

The paper's own case study is the most honest thing in it. From 2007 to 2017, the slow gains-budget approach could not diversify Apple — the stock rose so fast that after ten years it was still eighty percent of the position. The fast long/short program had Apple down to about one percent within two years. With hindsight, the Apple holder would call that rapid diversification a calamity. The authors' point, and it is the point of this entire video: Apple's run was a rare event, impossible to forecast at the start. You do not get to know which stock you are holding. You get to choose how much of your future depends on finding out.

`[BEAT]` And the leverage is not free. Financing costs erode returns. Elm Wealth's twenty-year model found fees consumed more than half the tax benefit. This is a tool for sophisticated investors who can tolerate leverage, complexity, and a large exit tax — and who have the scale to negotiate the fee line down, because at this level the fee line matters more than the strategy line.

`[VISUAL: "WRONG ANSWER" card]`

Kill conditions: you are leverage-averse. You are fee-sensitive. You cannot tolerate the complexity or the exit-tax lump. Then the simple sale — the benchmark this whole video keeps returning to — is your answer, and there is no shame in it.
